## The I-70 mountain corridor

Clear Creek County is the stretch of I-70 west of the foothills — about 9,400 people spread across four historic mining towns on the valley floors and unincorporated mountain subdivisions above them, from roughly 7,500 feet at Idaho Springs to 10,500 feet at the top of the county. The unincorporated side is places like Floyd Hill, Saint Mary's Glacier, York Gulch, Alvarado Road, Dumont, and Lawson: homes on steep, rocky multi-acre lots where the deck is often the best square footage on the property. Everything here is engineered for snow first.

## Where we build in Clear Creek County

[Idaho Springs](/locations/idaho-springs) is the biggest of the four towns and the closest to us, a canyon town where most decks are elevated over steep grade. [Georgetown](/locations/georgetown) is the county seat and a National Historic Landmark district, where the Design Review Commission signs off on exterior work before the permit. [Silver Plume](/locations/silver-plume) is the highest and smallest, a two-block silver camp at 9,100 feet that issues its own permits. [Empire](/locations/empire) sits on US 40 toward Berthoud Pass, where we confirm the permit path with Town Hall before drawing anything. And the unincorporated county — Floyd Hill on the [Evergreen](/locations/evergreen) side, Saint Mary's Glacier, Dumont and Lawson along the interstate — permits through the county itself.

## Permits: one county counter, four town counters

The Clear Creek County Building Department at 1111 Rose Street in Georgetown issues permits for unincorporated addresses, with applications through its GovBuilt portal. The four towns each issue their own: Idaho Springs through City Hall with SAFEbuilt doing plan review; Georgetown through Building Services, also with SAFEbuilt, after a Certificate of Appropriateness from the Design Review Commission; Silver Plume through the Town Clerk and Planning & Zoning Board on its own 2026 application; and Empire, which publishes no building-permit form — we confirm town or county with the Clerk. The county side has rules worth knowing before you start: it adopted the 2024 I-Codes in 2026 under Resolution R-26-19, every structural deck plan must be wet-stamped by an engineer regardless of who builds it, homeowners may build their own deck but must meet the county's deck checklist, and the office works a 32-hour week, so check hours before visiting. We confirm the right counter for your parcel, prepare one packet, and manage inspections through final.

## Frost at 42 inches, snow by ClearMap, embers by code

The county's design criteria are specific. Frost line is a 42-inch minimum, with deck piers at least 36 inches below grade — deeper than the 30 inches most of the metro builds to. Seismic Design Category is B. Roof snow load is not a single county figure; it is looked up by elevation on the county's Snow Load Design Table through ClearMap, parcel by parcel, and wind loads live on the same map. Every [covered deck or pergola](/services/pergolas-patio-covers) we build here is engineered to those numbers. The county also requires 2025 Colorado Wildfire Resiliency Code compliance with every permit, plus a Defensible Space Site Plan showing the 0–5, 5–30, and 30–100-plus foot zones, and sprinklers in all new single-family homes. We detail decks for embers — screened framing bays, clear undersides, [capped composite](/services/composite-decks) surfaces — because the code asks and the terrain deserves it.

## Repair, replace, and the honest answer

A lot of corridor decks were built before the current loads were written down, on cabins and mining-era houses. We inspect the frame first and answer plainly: sound structure gets a composite [re-deck](/services/deck-repair); aged-out structure gets a [replacement](/services/deck-replacement) built to the stamped plans the county now requires. Either way it is in writing.
