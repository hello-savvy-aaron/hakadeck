## Two blocks wide, 9,118 feet up

Silver Plume is the highest of the Clear Creek towns and the smallest — about 180 people in an 1870s silver camp squeezed between I-70 and the mountainside, the terminus of the Georgetown Loop Railroad. Main Street is the historic district; Woodward, Brownell, and Mountain Street climb toward the base of Sunrise Peak. Nearly every house is a small miner's frame cottage on a narrow lot, and many of those lots end in a steep rear yard. That is where the deck usually goes, which makes it a structural project — stepped footings, taller posts, railing at height — before it is an outdoor room.

## The town issues its own permits

Silver Plume runs its own building permits, on paper and email. You download the 2026 Building Permit Application and fee schedule from the town's Licenses & Permits page and submit it to the Town Clerk at Small Town Hall, 710 Main Street; the Planning & Zoning Board reviews applications and meets as needed at 7 pm, and building-permit inquiries go to the town's planning email. The town notes an online application is coming, but for now it is a paper process, and a small-town board that meets as needed sets its own pace — we build that into the schedule honestly rather than promising a metro turnaround. Silver Plume is also part of the Georgetown–Silver Plume National Historic Landmark District, so exterior changes fall under town planning review; we prepare drawings and material specs with that in mind. Parcels outside town limits permit through [Clear Creek County](/locations/clear-creek-county) in Georgetown.

## Snow at the top of the county

The town publishes no design-load table, so the county's design criteria are the reference: a 42-inch minimum frost line, deck piers at least 36 inches below grade, and a roof snow load looked up by elevation on the county's ClearMap table. At 9,100 feet, Silver Plume sits at the heavy end of that table. Every beam, post, and footing under a [covered deck](/services/pergolas-patio-covers) is sized to the parcel's number, and an open deck carries its share too — a winter's accumulation on the boards is a real load, not a nuisance. The county also enforces the 2025 Colorado Wildfire Resiliency Code with a defensible-space site plan; we confirm with the Clerk whether it applies inside town, and we detail for embers regardless — framing bays screened, undersides kept clear.

## Composite for a cottage that gets shoveled

A Silver Plume deck is shoveled for half the year and baked by thin-air sun for the other half. [Capped composite](/services/composite-decks) does not soak up meltwater before a freeze and does not need refinishing every summer, which is why we lead with it up here. On a narrow lot with a steep back yard, the deck is often the only usable outdoor space, and slim aluminum or cable [railing](/services/railings) keeps the mountainside in view instead of behind pickets.

## Old decks on old houses

Decks hung on 1870s cottages over the decades were framed to whatever the builder had on hand. We inspect before quoting. Sound framing earns a composite [re-deck](/services/deck-repair); an undersized or rotted frame gets a [replacement](/services/deck-replacement) on current-code structure, in writing either way. Rebuilding once beats repairing twice, and at this elevation the difference shows up the first winter.

## Seventy-five minutes, scheduled in blocks

Silver Plume is about 75 minutes from our Centennial shop — up I-70 past [Idaho Springs](/locations/idaho-springs) and one exit beyond [Georgetown](/locations/georgetown). We group mountain-corridor work so a crew stays on site for consecutive days rather than commuting for half-days, and we time footings around the short season at 9,000 feet; the drive shapes the calendar, not the price or the guarantee behind the work.
