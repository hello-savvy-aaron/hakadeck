  - q: "What does a new deck cost in Northglenn?"
    a: "Capped composite runs about $40–$70 per square foot installed, which puts a typical 300–400 square foot Northglenn deck around $15,000–$30,000 with railing and stairs. Replacing an existing deck lands in the same range unless the old footings can be verified and reused. Our online calculator gives you a planning range in about thirty seconds."
  - q: "My deck is original to my 1960s or 70s house — is it worth saving?"
    a: "Sometimes, and we will tell you honestly which case yours is. Decks from that era were usually built before modern ledger, footing, and hardware standards, so we inspect the connection to the house and the posts first — that is where old decks fail expensively. Sound framing can earn a re-deck in new composite for meaningfully less than starting over; framing that is done gets a proper replacement on current-code structure."
  - q: "Do you repair decks, or only replace them?"
    a: "Both, and the inspection decides which one we recommend — in writing, including when the cheaper answer is the right one. Loose railings, rotted stair stringers, and failing boards on sound framing are repairs. A ledger pulling off the house, heaved footings, or widespread frame rot make repair money badly spent, and we say so rather than patching what should be rebuilt."

## A suburb built in one generation

Northglenn went up fast and on purpose. Perl-Mack broke ground in 1959, and by 1962 it was called the most perfectly planned community in America. The result is one of the most consistent housing stocks in the metro: 1960s and 70s ranches and split-levels on modest lots, under trees that have had decades to mature.

When a whole city's homes are the same age, a whole city's decks age out together — and a large share of the original wood decks here are past the point where another coat of stain is an honest answer.

## The original-deck problem

A deck built in the 1970s predates most of what current code requires: properly flashed and bolted ledgers, footings below frost depth, hardware at every structural connection. Those gaps are why we look at the structure before we quote anything. Our [deck safety guide](/deck-safety-inspection-guide) walks through the same checks we make — the ledger connection especially, because that is where old decks fail suddenly.

## Repair or replace, honestly

The inspection produces one of two answers, and we put either one in writing. Framing that is still sound earns a [re-deck](/services/deck-repair): new capped composite and railings on the existing structure, for meaningfully less than starting over. Framing that is finished gets a [replacement](/services/deck-replacement) on current-code structure — on houses this age, the more common call. Our [replacement cost guide](/blog/deck-replacement-cost-denver) is a fair preview of the numbers.

## Why composite, specifically

Northglenn sits at about 5,360 feet on the open Front Range plain, which means the full Colorado punishment list: high-altitude UV, freeze-thaw, and hail severe enough that the city publishes its own guidance for homeowners. [Capped composite](/services/composite-decks) shrugs at all three and asks for soap and water instead of an annual refinishing weekend. Mature trees here drop everything they own onto a deck; a surface that cannot rot under wet leaf litter is worth having. We pair it with aluminum [railing systems](/services/railings) that will not need repainting.

## Permits, by the book

Deck permits in Northglenn go through the City of Northglenn Building Division, with plan review and inspections handled by SAFEbuilt. The city publishes a building guide for uncovered decks, so expectations at the counter are clear. Drawings, permit filing, and inspection management are always part of our build, never your homework. The [permit guide](/deck-permits-south-denver-metro) covers the general process.
