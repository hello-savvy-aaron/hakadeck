  - q: "My deck is original to my 1960s or 70s house — is it worth saving?"
    a: "Sometimes, and we will tell you honestly which case yours is. Decks from that era were usually built before modern ledger, footing, and hardware standards, so we inspect the connection to the house and the posts first — that is where old decks fail expensively. Sound framing can earn a re-deck in new composite for meaningfully less than starting over; framing that is done gets a proper replacement on current-code structure."
  - q: "Do you repair decks, or only replace them?"
    a: "Both, and the inspection decides which one we recommend — in writing, including when the cheaper answer is the right one. Loose railings, rotted stair stringers, and failing boards on sound framing are repairs. A ledger pulling off the house, heaved footings, or widespread frame rot make repair money badly spent, and we say so rather than patching what should be rebuilt."

## A suburb built in one generation

Northglenn went up fast and on purpose. Perl-Mack broke ground in 1959, and by 1962 the development was being called the most perfectly planned community in America. The result, sixty-plus years on, is one of the most consistent housing stocks in the metro: 1960s and 70s ranches and split-levels on modest lots, under trees that have had decades to mature.

That consistency has a consequence for us. When a whole city's homes are the same age, a whole city's decks age out together — and a large share of the original wood decks here are past the point where another coat of stain is an honest answer.

## The original-deck problem

A deck built in the 1970s predates most of what current code requires: properly flashed and bolted ledgers, footings below frost depth, hardware at every structural connection. Age plus those gaps is why we look at the structure before we quote anything. Our [deck safety guide](/deck-safety-inspection-guide) walks through the same checks we make — the ledger connection especially, because that is where old decks fail suddenly rather than gracefully.

## Repair or replace, honestly

The inspection produces one of two answers, and we put either one in writing. Framing that is still sound earns a [re-deck](/services/deck-repair): new capped composite and railings on the existing structure, for meaningfully less than starting over. Framing that is finished gets a full [replacement](/services/deck-replacement) on current-code structure — and on houses this age, that is the more common call. Our [replacement cost guide](/blog/deck-replacement-cost-denver) is a fair preview of the numbers.

## Why composite, specifically

Northglenn sits at about 5,360 feet on the open Front Range plain, which means the full Colorado punishment list: high-altitude UV, hard freeze-thaw cycling, and hail seasons serious enough that the city publishes its own hail guidance for homeowners. [Capped composite](/services/composite-decks) shrugs at all three and asks for soap and water instead of an annual refinishing weekend. The mature trees here are lovely and also drop everything they own onto your deck; a surface that cannot rot under wet leaf litter is worth having. We pair it with aluminum [railing systems](/services/railings) that will not need repainting either.

## Permits, by the book

Deck permits in Northglenn go through the City of Northglenn Building Division, with plan review and inspections handled by SAFEbuilt. The city publishes a building guide for uncovered decks, so expectations at the counter are clear — and we meet them. Drawings, permit filing, and inspection management are always part of our build, never your homework. The [permit guide](/deck-permits-south-denver-metro) covers the general process if you want the background.
