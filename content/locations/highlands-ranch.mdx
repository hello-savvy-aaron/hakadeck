## Built for how Highlands Ranch actually sits

Highlands Ranch rolls. The master-planned community grew across genuinely hilly terrain from the 1980s through the 2000s, which is why so many of its homes have walk-out or garden-level basements — and why so many of its decks stand a full story or more off the ground. Elevated decks are structural projects first and outdoor rooms second: taller posts, engineered footings, and railing systems that have to be right. That's the work we do most, and the reason homeowners looking for a decking contractor in Highlands Ranch — not just someone to lay boards on a flat lot — end up finding us.

## The HRCA is not optional — and that's fine

Nearly every home in Highlands Ranch sits under the Highlands Ranch Community Association, and the HRCA's architectural review committee looks at any deck, pergola, or patio cover before a single post goes in. Submittal drawings, material and color specs, site plans — we prepare and file all of it as part of every Highlands Ranch build, then run the [Douglas County building permit](/blog/deck-permits-centennial-arapahoe-douglas) and inspections alongside it. Homeowners who've been through HRCA review solo tell us the paperwork was the worst part of their last project. It isn't part of yours; it's part of ours.

## What we replace here

The first generation of Highlands Ranch decks — redwood and cedar builds from the '80s and '90s — is aging out all at once. South-facing spans at 5,900 feet take a beating from sun and freeze-thaw, and a lot of original railings no longer meet code. The typical Haka project here is a full teardown and [capped composite rebuild](/services/composite-decks), often adding a [pergola or covered section](/services/pergolas-patio-covers) so the new deck is usable through more of the year. If the framing turns out to be sound, we'll tell you and quote a [re-deck](/services/deck-repair) instead — the honest answer is cheaper more often than people expect.

## Deck repair and railings, without the rebuild

Not every call needs a teardown. Deck repair in Highlands Ranch is a steady share of our work: re-securing ledgers, replacing rotted posts and beams, tightening or replacing stairs, and swapping storm-damaged boards on decks whose structure is still worth saving. We inspect first, photograph what we find, and quote the repair honestly against the cost of replacement — sometimes a few thousand dollars of structural work buys another decade.

Deck railings in Highlands Ranch are the other standalone job. A lot of railings here from the '90s are wood two-by verticals — sun-checked, loose at the posts, and under today's 200-pound load standard the first thing an inspector flags. We replace them with [cable, aluminum, or composite systems](/services/railings) on decks we didn't build, matched to HRCA color standards and anchored into blocking rather than just the rim board. New railing changes how a deck looks more than any other single upgrade, and on walk-out lots it changes the view too.

## Deck railings in Highlands Ranch

On a walk-out lot, the railing is what you look through every time you look out. That's why deck railings in Highlands Ranch are so often a standalone project: the deck itself is fine, but the wood two-by-twos that came with the house chop the backdrop into slats and demand a staining schedule nobody keeps. Cable rail all but disappears against the view, and slim-post aluminum comes close while needing a rinse instead of a refinish — on an elevated deck looking west, either one is the difference between a fence and a frame.

The code numbers are worth knowing before you shop. Any deck surface more than 30 inches off the ground — which describes most of Highlands Ranch — needs a guardrail at least 36 inches high, with no opening large enough to pass a 4-inch sphere, and a top rail built to resist a 200-pound load in any direction. A lot of original railings here fail at least one of those three, and the load requirement is the one you can't see: a post that looks solid but flexes when a kid leans on it is exactly what an inspector — or a home inspector at resale — will flag.

A railing swap is also one of the few deck projects that fits neatly into fall. Most run one to two days on site — no footings, no long permit review, no torn-up yard — so it's a project you can finish between now and the first snow instead of carrying into spring. If you're weighing the options, our [railing systems page](/services/railings) walks through cable, aluminum, and composite side by side, and our guide to [what railing replacement costs](/blog/deck-railing-replacement-cost) puts real per-linear-foot numbers on each.
