faqs:
  - q: "How should a deck be built for wildfire risk in Evergreen?"
    a: "Deliberately, because a deck is one of the most ember-exposed assemblies on a mountain house. Embers land on the surface, work into the framing bays underneath, and ignite whatever is stored below. Building fire-wise means composite or mineral-based surfaces rather than untreated softwood, framing bays closed or screened so embers can't collect, no combustible storage designed into the underside, and attention to what's growing within the first few feet of the structure. We confirm the current requirements for your specific parcel with Jefferson County before detailing."
  - q: "Does mountain snow load change the structure?"
    a: "Substantially, and it's the opposite problem from the plains. Up here a roofed structure has to carry serious accumulated snow — mountain design loads run well above metro requirements — so beams, posts, and footings are all sized larger for a covered deck than the same span would need in Denver. A cover engineered for a suburban lot is not safe at this elevation, which is a real risk with a builder who works mostly downhill."
  - q: "What's involved in building on a steep, rocky lot?"
    a: "More engineering and more labor than a flat build. Shallow bedrock and decomposed granite are common here, so footings sometimes get drilled and pinned rather than augered, and steep access means materials get staged and moved by hand more often than by machine. We identify all of that during the walkthrough — the worst version of this job is discovering the site conditions after the quote is signed."
  - q: "What does a mountain deck cost in Evergreen?"
    a: "Above metro pricing, and the difference is real structure rather than markup. The baseline for capped composite is about $40–$70 per square foot installed; mountain builds add for engineered snow-load framing, harder footing work, steep-site staging, and railing at height. Most Evergreen projects we quote land between $25,000 and $60,000, itemized so you can see exactly what the mountain is adding."

## Actual mountain building

Evergreen is where our work stops being suburban. Lots are steep, bedrock sits close to the surface, the snow that lands on a roof in December is still there in February, and the forest that makes the place beautiful also makes wildfire a permanent consideration — all of which is why a builder who works exclusively on the metro floor is a genuine risk up here, not a mere mismatch.

## Fire-wise, by default

A deck on a mountain lot is one of the most ember-exposed parts of the house. Embers settle on the surface, drift into the framing bays underneath, and find whatever's been stacked down there over the years.

So the detailing is deliberate: [composite or mineral-based boards](/services/composite-decks) instead of untreated softwood, framing bays closed or screened so embers can't nest below the deck, no combustible storage designed into the underside, and honest attention to what's growing in the first few feet around the structure. Where your parcel carries specific ignition-resistant requirements, we confirm them with Jefferson County and detail to them before drawing.

These choices won't make a deck fireproof, but they meaningfully reduce a known risk and cost far less to build in than to retrofit — the same approach we take on the foothills edge in [Golden](/locations/golden) and [Boulder](/locations/boulder), applied to steeper, more wooded ground.

## Snow is the load that governs

On the plains, the design problem for a covered structure is wind pulling upward; up here it's the opposite — mountain snow loads run well above metro requirements, and a [patio cover or roofed section](/services/pergolas-patio-covers) has to carry a heavy accumulation that lingers for months. That means larger beams, more posts, and bigger footings than the same span would need in Denver — the most common thing builders get wrong on mountain covers, and it isn't cosmetic: it's the kind of error that fails under two feet of wet spring snow.

## Rock, slope, and access

Shallow bedrock and decomposed granite show up regularly on Evergreen lots, so footings occasionally need drilling and pinning rather than augering. Steep sites mean materials get staged and often moved by hand where no machine can reach. Both affect the schedule and the labor, and both are things we'd far rather establish at the walkthrough than discover on day two.

Elevated decks on mountain lots are the best rooms in the house, and [railing](/services/railings) choice — cable or slim aluminum — keeps the trees and ridgeline in view instead of behind a picket screen.

## Aging mountain decks

Plenty of Evergreen homes carry a deck built decades ago, and mountain weather is harder on a structure than the calendar suggests — sun at elevation, snow sitting on it for months, and moisture cycling through the framing every spring. We inspect before quoting, starting at the ledger where the deck meets the house.

Sometimes the framing is genuinely sound and a [re-deck](/services/deck-repair) in fire-wise composite is the honest, cheaper answer. Sometimes an old structure was never sized for the snow loads it's been carrying, in which case a full [replacement](/services/deck-replacement) isn't an upsell — it's the finding. We put either conclusion in writing.

## Permits through the county

Evergreen is unincorporated Jefferson County, so there's no separate city counter — the county handles your permit, and its mountain requirements differ from what applies down on the plains. Some communities here also run covenants with architectural review.

We produce the drawings to the county's standard, file the [permit](/deck-permits-south-denver-metro), prepare any covenant packet, and manage inspections through final sign-off.
