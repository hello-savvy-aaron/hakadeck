Edgewater lots are narrow, which keeps footprints modest, but the 36-inch footing requirement and tight access between houses add labor, and a second-story deck on a new duplex is real structure. ## A square mile beside Sloan's Lake

Edgewater is one square mile of city on the west shore of Sloan's Lake, at about 5,380 feet, with roughly 5,100 residents. The housing is mostly 1900s–1950s bungalows and small brick ranches on narrow lots, and the blocks around the 25th Avenue business district, the Harlan Street corridor, and Edgewater Public Market are changing fast through scrape-and-rebuild and duplex infill. That mix is why we get two very different calls here: an old house that has never had a proper deck, and a new duplex whose second-story deck is the best room in the unit.

## The city publishes its numbers

Edgewater does something most metro cities do not: it posts its design criteria. Snow load is 40 psf, wind is 130 mph Ultimate at Exposure B, and frost depth is 36 inches, with a minimum footing depth of 36 inches below finished grade, all under the 2021 International codes and the 2020 NEC. We size every beam, post, and footing to those figures. The wind number matters most on a [covered deck or patio cover](/services/pergolas-patio-covers), where uplift on the roof governs the connections, and the 36-inch footing is deeper than the 30-inch floor some neighboring jurisdictions accept. Having the criteria in writing makes plan review cleaner, because there is nothing to interpret.

## SAFEbuilt handles the counter

Permits go through the City of Edgewater's Building & Planning office, with building services contracted to SAFEbuilt since 2013 and plans also reviewed by West Metro Fire Rescue District. Zoning and setback questions go to the city's Planning & Development Manager, which matters on narrow lots where the side setback decides whether a deck can wrap the house. This is a city counter, not [Jefferson County](/locations/jefferson-county)'s. We confirm the setbacks, prepare drawings to the city's criteria, file, and run inspections through sign-off. Our [permit guide](/deck-permits-south-denver-metro) covers the general process.

## Narrow lots, close neighbors

A 1920s bungalow lot leaves little room, and the deck often has to thread between the house and the fence. We design from the actual setbacks and think about what the neighbors see: on a tight block the railing is most of the view, and slim aluminum or cable keeps a small deck from feeling boxed in. On new duplex builds the second-story deck is usually a structural project first — taller posts, engineered footings, code railing at height — and an outdoor room second.

## Composite, because the weather is the same here

Hail, high-altitude UV, and freeze-thaw work on an Edgewater deck exactly as they do everywhere on the Front Range. [Capped composite](/services/composite-decks) holds its color and needs a rinse rather than a stain, which is why we lead with it; we install Trex, Deckorators, and TimberTech as a certified dealer. Where a homeowner wants the look of real wood on a 1920s house, we will say honestly what that costs in upkeep.

## Repair or replace

Decks on Edgewater's older homes were often added long after the house, to whatever standard the builder knew. We inspect first. Sound framing earns a [re-deck](/services/deck-repair) at $20–$35 per square foot; shallow footings, rot at the posts, or a nailed ledger get a [replacement](/services/deck-replacement) recommendation, in writing. The inspection is free.
