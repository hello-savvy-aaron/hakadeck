## Platte Canyon at 7,740 feet

Bailey is where US-285 drops into the Platte Canyon — about 8,000 people spread across the townsite and the subdivisions that climb out of it: Burland Ranchettes, Harris Park, Will-O-Wisp, Elk Creek Highlands, Deer Creek Valley Ranchos. The housing is 1960s-to-2000s mountain homes and cabins on one-to-five-acre lots between 7,700 and 9,000 feet — steep, wooded, with heavy snow and winters that run long. A deck here is a structure first: it has to carry a mountain snowpack, survive freeze-thaw that works every fastener, and shrug off UV that strips a wood finish in a season or two.

## Permits come from Fairplay

Bailey is unincorporated, so the Park County Building Department issues the permit from the county seat in Fairplay — 856 Castello Avenue, lobby open Monday through Thursday from 7 a.m. to 5 p.m., about forty-five minutes west. Nobody has to make that drive to file: applications run through the county's CloudPermit portal. The county has adopted the 2018 IRC and directs Bailey-area projects to the Platte Canyon Fire Protection District for fire-mitigation requirements, which we fold into the plan rather than treating as an afterthought. We prepare one clean packet, file it, and manage inspections through final. Our permit guide covers the general process, and our [Park County](/locations/park-county) page covers the county.

## Loads from the hazard tool, not a lookup table

Park County does not publish a single snow-load number. Since January 1, 2024, the county requires every set of plans to use ground snow load and ultimate wind speed from the ASCE Hazard Tool under ASCE/SEI 7-22, and it publishes a snow-load map at maps.parkco.us. The figure is parcel-specific and climbs with elevation, so a [covered deck or patio cover](/services/pergolas-patio-covers) in Elk Creek Highlands is engineered for more than one in the townsite. We pull the numbers for your address, size beams, posts, and footings to them, and set footings below Colorado's 30–36 inch frost line. Our post on how much snow a deck can hold explains what those numbers mean in lumber.

## What we build in the canyon

Acreage lots in Bailey want more deck than a subdivision would — often a walkout on a steep grade with a covered section so it works through the winter, and railing that keeps the canyon in view. We lead with [capped composite](/services/composite-decks) because this is the weather it was made for: altitude UV, deep freeze-thaw, wet spring snow. It also does not add flammable mass to the edge of the house, which matters when the fire district is reviewing your mitigation. We frame fire-aware as a matter of habit — bays screened against embers, undersides kept clear of stored fuel. The covered deck cost guide has honest numbers for the roofed version.

## Repair or replace, at 8,000 feet

Many Bailey decks date to the 1960s and 70s, built to lighter snow assumptions than the hazard tool now returns. We inspect the frame before quoting. Sound framing earns a [re-deck](/services/deck-repair) at $20–$35 per square foot over the existing structure; framing that cannot carry the parcel's load gets a [replacement](/services/deck-replacement) on current-code structure. The recommendation comes in writing either way, and the deck safety inspection guide shows what we look at.
