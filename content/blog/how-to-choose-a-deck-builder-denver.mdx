"Best deck builder near me" turns up a page of five-star reviews and confident-sounding homepages, and none of it tells you much. Here's what we'd actually check — the signals that are real and verifiable rather than the ones that just sound good.

## The checklist at a glance

| What to check | A good answer looks like | Walk away when |
|---|---|---|
| License & insurance | Registered in *your* jurisdiction; COI from the agent showing $1M+ liability and workers' comp | "We're fully covered" with no paperwork |
| Certification tier | A named tier — Trex Platinum Pro, Deckorators Pro Elite, TimberTech dealer | Can't name one |
| Workmanship warranty | A written term covering the structure, not just boards | "We stand behind our work" |
| Permits | "We pull it — drawings, submission, inspections" | "Skip it to save time" |
| Bid format | Itemized: permits, demo, footings, framing, decking, railing, stairs | One lump-sum number |
| Payment schedule | 10–30% deposit, milestone payments, balance at final inspection | Half down |
| Lien protection | Signed lien waivers with each progress payment | A blank stare at the word "waiver" |

The rest of this post is the *why* behind each row — and how to verify without taking anyone's word for it.

## Licensed and insured — and willing to prove it

Colorado has a quirk: there is **no statewide general-contractor license**. Licensing happens city by city and county by county, so "licensed" only means something in the jurisdiction your house sits in — and a two-minute call to your building department confirms whether a builder is actually on their list.

Insurance is where the numbers matter. Ask for a **certificate of insurance (COI)** sent directly from the contractor's insurance agent — not a photocopy from the builder — and check three things:

- **General liability of at least $1 million per occurrence** ($2 million aggregate is the common pairing for structural work).
- **Workers' compensation.** Colorado requires it with even one employee — without it, an injured crew member's claim can land on your homeowner's policy.
- **The name on the certificate matches the name on your contract.**

Ask before you sign, not after — a professional's agent can email a COI the same day.

## Manufacturer certification tiers, and what they actually unlock

Composite and PVC brands run installer certification programs — Trex Platinum Pro, Deckorators Pro Elite, TimberTech authorized dealer. These aren't marketing badges: on some product lines the tier directly extends the **labor-coverage** period of your warranty — the part that pays for reinstallation, not just replacement boards. A contractor who can't name their tier probably doesn't have one, which means the installation itself carries no manufacturer backing at all.

## A written workmanship warranty with a real term

"We stand behind our work" isn't a warranty; it's a sentence. Ask for the term in writing — one year, two years, longer — and what it actually covers: framing, fasteners, and connections, or just the surface. Then ask how a claim has actually played out for a past customer. A contractor who's proud of their warranty will tell you the story; one who's never had to honor it might not have one worth much.

## Who pulls the permit

The fastest way to separate a professional operation from a handshake crew. A permitted deck means a third party — the building department — has independently checked the footings, framing, and railings against code; an unpermitted deck is a standing liability and a documented red flag when you sell. "Skip the permit to save time" isn't a shortcut — it's a sign nothing on the job gets checked by anyone but the builder. [What Front Range permits actually involve](/deck-permits-south-denver-metro) is worth reading regardless of who you hire.

## An itemized bid, not a lump sum

A single number — "$22,000, all in" — can't be compared to anything and gives a change order nowhere honest to land. An itemized bid breaks out plans and permits, demolition, footings, framing, decking, railing, and stairs, so two bids can be held side by side — the only format that reveals whether "the cheap bid" is cheaper or just missing a line item. Our [cost guide](/deck-cost-guide-denver) shows a real itemized bid, line by line.

## Deposit terms and lien waivers

The Colorado norm is a **10–30% deposit** covering materials mobilization, milestone payments as work completes, and the balance held until the final walkthrough. A builder asking for **50% up front** is asking you to carry the job's risk before a post hole exists.

Two items worth writing into the contract:

- **Lien waivers.** In Colorado, subs and suppliers can file a mechanic's lien against *your home* if the general contractor doesn't pay them — even when you've paid the GC in full. A signed lien waiver with each progress payment closes that door. Professional builders produce these routinely.
- **Final payment after final inspection.** Tie the last check to the building department's sign-off, not the builder's. It costs nothing and aligns every incentive with a passed inspection.

## Local specialization, not just local address

A contractor can be headquartered five minutes from your house and still build decks the way they'd build them in Phoenix. What matters is whether the structure is engineered for where you live: footings below the Front Range frost line, framing sized for real snow load, fasteners specified for freeze-thaw, and roof connections engineered for wind uplift rather than copied from a flatland detail. Ask what changes about their build for Colorado specifically — a vague answer is the answer.

## Reviews you can actually verify

A five-star average is a start, not a finding. Look for reviews detailed enough to verify — a neighborhood, a build type, a timeline. Better still, ask for two or three addresses of recent local work you can drive past, and permission to ask those homeowners how the year since the build has gone. Reputable builders offer this without flinching.

## The one-year test

The best signal of all is the one almost nobody thinks to ask about: does the builder come back after the first winter? A deck's real test in Colorado isn't the day it's finished — it's the first hard freeze-thaw cycle, the first heavy snow load, the first summer of full UV. A contractor who checks back a year later, unprompted, is telling you something about how they expect the work to hold up. One who disappears after the final payment is telling you something too.
