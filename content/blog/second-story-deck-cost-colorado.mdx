Half the homes in Highlands Ranch, Parker, and Castle Rock were built on terrain that rolls — which means walk-out basements, and decks that stand a full story off the ground. If that's your house, every generic deck-cost article you've read is quietly wrong for you. Height changes the math, and here's how.

## The short answer

For a capped-composite elevated deck in the Denver metro in 2026, plan on **20–40% above ground-level pricing** — call it **$48–$98 per square foot installed**, against $40–$70 for the same deck at grade. Typical projects:

| Project | Typical installed cost |
|---|---|
| 300 sq ft walk-out deck | $14,500–$29,500 |
| 400 sq ft second-story deck with stairs | $21,000–$44,000 |
| Two-tier build (upper deck + finished lower patio) | $35,000–$65,000+ |

A worked comparison makes the multiplier concrete: a 16×20 (320 sq ft) composite deck at grade runs **$13,000–$25,000** installed. Lift the identical deck to walk-out height and it prices at roughly **$15,500–$31,500** — same boards, same footprint, different structure.

## Where the extra money goes

None of it is markup for altitude drama — it's structure you can point at:

- **Posts and beams.** A deck ten feet up carries the same loads through much longer posts — 6×6 minimum, often bigger — which means larger beams, more bracing, and connections engineered for the leverage.
- **Footings.** Taller structure concentrates bigger loads on each footing. Every one is dug to Colorado frost depth (30–36 inches) regardless, at **$150–$600 apiece** — elevated decks just demand larger diameters, and there's more riding on each.
- **Railings that are truly fall protection.** At second-story height, the railing isn't trim — it's the thing between a guest and a ten-foot drop. Code requires a guardrail on anything over 30 inches: 36 inches minimum height, no gap that passes a 4-inch sphere, built to resist a 200-pound load at the top rail. Installed railing runs **$30–$90 per linear foot**, and elevated decks need every foot of it.
- **Stairs to grade.** A staircase from a second-story deck is a small structure in its own right — a story of height at the code-max 7¾-inch rise is 13–14 steps, with stringers, a landing, its own footings and railing. At **$150–$400 per step**, budget **$2,000–$4,500+** depending on the run; our [stairs and railings guide](/blog/deck-stairs-railings-cost-guide) goes deeper.
- **Labor and access.** Everything happens off ladders and scaffolds, slower and more carefully than deck work at grade — height is the biggest single line in the [+20–40% multiplier](/deck-cost-guide-denver), and it stacks with tight access (+10–15%) and complex shapes (+15–25%).

Splitting that 320 sq ft example open shows the premium isn't one line — it's every line getting heavier:

| Line item | At grade | At walk-out height |
|---|---|---|
| Posts, beams, bracing | Standard 4×4/6×6, minimal bracing | Long 6×6+, engineered bracing |
| Footings (each) | $150–$400 | $300–$600, larger diameter |
| Stairs | 2–4 steps: $300–$1,600 | 13–14 steps + landing: $2,000–$5,600 |
| Railing | Optional below 30" of height | Code-required, every open edge |
| Labor | At grade | Ladders and scaffolds throughout |

One more load note while you're up there: if a hot tub is anywhere in the plan, say so at design time. A filled 7-foot tub weighs around 5,000 pounds — over 100 pounds per square foot against the 40 psf a standard deck is framed for — and on an elevated deck that's a beam-and-footing redesign, not a tweak. Our [hot tub deck guide](/blog/hot-tub-deck-guide) covers the framing.

## The walk-out bonus: the space underneath

Here's the part that flips the math back in your favor: an elevated deck creates a shaded, rain-protected room under it for a fraction of what that square footage costs anywhere else on your property. An under-deck drainage-and-ceiling system runs roughly **$8–$20 per square foot installed** — against $40–$70 for new deck space or far more for anything with a real roof. Add a gravel or paver pad, a fan and lights, and the deck you bought for upstairs dining doubles your usable outdoor space. Our [two-tier builds](/portfolio/two-tier-balcony-deck) and [covered walk-out projects](/portfolio/walkout-covered-deck) are the pattern: dining up top, lounge in the shade below. It's the single best value-add in elevated deck design, and it's why we rarely build a walk-out deck without at least roughing in the space beneath.

## Engineering isn't optional at height

An undersized ground-level deck sags; an undersized second-story deck is a genuine hazard. Every elevated deck clears the permit threshold automatically — attached, and well over 30 inches above grade — which means engineered drawings, framing sized for the Front Range's 30 psf design snow load, and three inspections (footing holes, rough framing, final). Permits run **$150–$500** in the south metro with a 1–3 week turnaround, and elevated decks are exactly where they earn their keep — and where builder-grade shortcuts from the '90s show up worst. Every Haka elevated build is [permitted and inspected](/blog/deck-permits-centennial-arapahoe-douglas), designed for Colorado wind and snow loads, and built like the fall protection it is.

## Should you build at height at all?

Two honest decision rules. If your door is at grade and you're choosing height for looks, don't — staying low avoids the entire 20–40% premium, and a ground-level deck under 30 inches skips the guardrail requirement besides. But if the house hands you a walk-out or a second-story door, the math already made the call: the deck belongs at the door, and the covered room underneath is how you claw the premium back.
