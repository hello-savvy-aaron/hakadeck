Homeowners across Centennial, Highlands Ranch, and Lone Tree ask us the same first question: *what's a composite deck going to run me?* The honest answer is that it depends on size, height, and how the yard sits — but "it depends" is a useless answer when you're trying to budget. So here are real 2026 numbers for the Denver metro, what moves them, and how to read a bid so you know what you're actually paying for.

## The short answer

For a professionally built capped-composite deck in the Denver metro, expect **$40–$70 per square foot installed** in 2026. That's the all-in number — materials, substructure, fasteners, labor, permits, and cleanup. Run against the most common deck sizes:

| Deck size | Materials alone | Typical installed cost |
|---|---|---|
| 12×16 (192 sq ft) | $4,500–$6,500 | $8,000–$13,000 |
| 16×20 (320 sq ft) | $7,000–$10,500 | $13,000–$25,000 |
| 20×24 (480 sq ft) | $10,500–$15,500 | $19,000–$37,000 |
| 700+ sq ft (multi-level / wraparound) | — | $30,000–$55,000+ |

For calibration: the 2025 Zonda Cost vs Value report puts the national-average 16×20 composite deck addition at about **$25,000**, with roughly **68% recouped at resale**. Denver runs a little above the national average, and there's a real reason for it: our builds carry heavier structural requirements than decks in milder climates (more on that below).

## Where the money actually goes

People assume the boards are most of the cost. They're a fifth of it. Composite boards themselves run **$8–$14 per sq ft**; all-in materials land around **$20–$30 per sq ft**; professional installation adds **$15–$35 per sq ft** on top — labor is roughly 40–60% of the finished number. On a typical composite deck the bill splits about like this:

- **Substructure and its labor (~38%)** — footings, posts, beams, and joists. This is the part nobody sees and the part that determines whether your deck is still solid in 20 years. In Colorado this is where cost gets added, not saved.
- **Decking and railing labor (~30%)** — framing the surface, board install, and finish work at code height and spacing.
- **Decking boards and fasteners (~20%)** — the composite surface and matching fascia.
- **Railing material (~12%)** — and the variable line item that swings a bid the most.

## What drives your number up or down

**Height.** The biggest multiplier on the list: a walk-out or second-story deck runs **+20–40%** over the same deck at grade — taller posts, bigger footings, engineered railing loads, and often a full staircase at **$150–$400 per step** ($1,500–$4,000 for a typical run). Our [second-story deck cost guide](/blog/second-story-deck-cost-colorado) works the math.

**Railing choice.** The single biggest swing after size and height. Installed railing runs **$30–$90 per linear foot** depending on style — on a deck with 60 linear feet, that spread alone is **$3,600**, and glass panels can push past the top of it. Premium rail on the view side, standard aluminum elsewhere, is the honest compromise.

**Board tier.** Entry composite (Trex Enhance, TimberTech Edge) sits at the low end of $40–$70. Premium capped and PVC lines (TimberTech AZEK, Deckorators Voyage) push toward the **$50–$85** PVC/mineral-board range — worth it for cooler surface temps and 25–50 year warranties, which matter in our sun. We break down the tradeoffs in our [Trex vs. TimberTech vs. Fiberon comparison](/blog/trex-vs-timbertech-vs-fiberon).

**Site conditions.** Tight access — side yards a machine can't reach — adds **10–15%**. Sloped lots (common in Castle Rock, Ken Caryl, and the foothills-adjacent neighborhoods) and rocky soil add engineering.

**Shape and levels.** Rectangles are cheap. Angles, curves, multiple tiers, and picture-frame borders add **15–25%** in cutting, waste, and framing hours. These multipliers stack — it's how two 320 sq ft decks can honestly bid at $14,000 and $27,000.

## Why Denver decks cost a bit more to build right

Colorado's climate isn't a marketing line — it's a line item. A deck here has to survive UV roughly 25% stronger than sea level, summer hail, and 35–90 freeze-thaw cycles a year that work fasteners loose and heave poorly-set footings. Built correctly, that means:

- **Deeper footings** poured below the frost line — 30–36 inches in our metro — so the deck doesn't shift as the ground freezes and thaws.
- **Corrosion-rated hardware and hidden fasteners** that won't back out over years of thermal movement.
- **Joists at 16 inches on center with proper flashing** — composite's span requirement, and the detail that keeps snowmelt draining instead of pooling against the house.

A cheaper bid that skips these isn't a better deal — it's a deck that needs rework in five years. When you compare quotes, the substructure spec is where you find out who's actually building for this climate. Our [composite deck page](/services/composite-decks) walks through how we frame for it.

## How to read a composite deck bid

A real bid should spell out, at minimum:

1. **Board brand, line, and color** — not just "composite."
2. **Substructure material and footing depth** — pressure-treated vs. steel, and how deep the footings go.
3. **Railing type and linear footage.**
4. **Whether permits and engineering are included** (they should be — figure $150–$500 in south-metro permit fees and three inspections).
5. **Warranty terms** — both the manufacturer's 25–50 years on the boards and the builder's on the labor.

If a quote is a single lump-sum number with none of that detail, you can't compare it to anything, and you can't hold anyone to it. Push for the line items — our [deck cost guide](/deck-cost-guide-denver) shows a real bid broken down line by line so you know what one should look like.

## Composite vs. wood — is the premium worth it here?

Upfront, composite costs more: a 320 sq ft pressure-treated deck runs about **$9,000** against **$16,000** in capped composite. Then the decade happens. Wood wants a **$400–$900** clean-and-restain every 2–3 years plus repairs as UV and freeze-thaw do their work — call it **$3,000–$5,000 over ten years** — while composite needs soap and water. The gap narrows to a few thousand dollars by year ten, and past it composite is simply ahead. We ran the full math in our [composite vs. wood guide](/blog/composite-vs-natural-decking-guide). For most homes in our service area, composite is the material we recommend — and it's not close.

## The honest bottom line

Budget **$13,000–$25,000** for a typical 16×20 backyard [composite deck in the Denver metro](/locations/denver), more if you're going tall, large, multi-level, or into premium railings. The cheapest bid is rarely the best value here, because the savings almost always come out of the substructure — the one part you can't see and can't easily fix later.
