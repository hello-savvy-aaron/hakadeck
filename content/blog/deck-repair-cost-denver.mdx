**Most Denver deck repairs cost between $300 and $2,500 in 2026, depending on whether the fix is cosmetic (a few boards) or structural (a ledger or footing). Small jobs are governed by service minimums of $300–$600; a full stair rebuild tops the common repairs at $1,500–$4,000.**

That's the short version. The useful version is itemized — because "deck repair" spans everything from swapping three boards to rebuilding the connection that holds the deck to your house, and the right decision depends on which one you're facing.

## What do specific deck repairs cost?

Real 2026 numbers for the Denver metro, installed:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service minimum (any small job) | $300–$600 |
| Board replacement (a handful of boards) | $150–$500 wood / $200–$600 composite-matched |
| Railing post re-anchor | $150–$400 per post |
| Joist sistering / repair | $150–$400 per joist |
| Ledger repair & re-flash | $800–$2,500 |
| Post + frost-depth footing replacement | $500–$1,200 each |
| Stair rebuild | $1,500–$4,000 |
| Hardware / fastener refresh | $300–$800 |
| Re-deck over sound framing | $20–$35 per sq ft |
| Full composite rebuild | $40–$70 per sq ft, plus $1,500–$3,500 tear-out |

Two notes on reading the table honestly. First, the **service minimum** is real: a crew, a truck, and materials cost the same to mobilize for one board as for ten, so almost no contractor will roll out for less than $300–$600. If you have three small annoyances — a wobbly rail, two cupped boards, a loose stair tread — bundle them into one visit and the minimum works *for* you instead of against you.

Second, composite board matching costs more than the material difference suggests. Discontinued colors, weathered fade on the surrounding boards, and hidden-fastener systems all add sourcing and labor time. It's still far cheaper than living with a soft spot.

## Why do the structural repairs cost so much more?

Because they're excavation and carpentry, not just carpentry.

A **ledger repair** ($800–$2,500) means removing deck boards and sometimes siding, cutting out rot, re-fastening with structural screws into the house rim, and re-flashing so water never gets back in. The ledger carries roughly half your deck's weight, which is why it's priced like the structural work it is — our [safety inspection guide](/deck-safety-inspection-guide) walks through how to spot ledger trouble yourself.

A **post and footing replacement** ($500–$1,200 each) involves temporarily shoring the deck, demoing the old footing, and pouring a new one to Colorado's **30–36-inch frost depth**. Shallower footings are exactly why so many older metro decks heave and lean — the freeze-thaw cycle lifts them a little every winter.

A **stair rebuild** ($1,500–$4,000) is its own small structure: footings under the stringers, new stringers, treads, and a code-compliant graspable handrail. Code is unforgiving here — risers within 7¾ inches and matching, treads at least 10 inches deep — and stairs built to earlier codes usually can't be patched into compliance. The full breakdown is in our [stairs and railings cost guide](/blog/deck-stairs-railings-cost-guide).

## Repair, re-deck, or replace?

The most expensive mistake in deck repair is fixing the wrong layer. Here's the decision logic we actually use on site visits:

- **Surface wear only** — faded, splintering, or cupped boards on a frame that's solid when you probe it → **re-deck**. New composite decking and railing over your existing framing runs **$20–$35 per square foot**, and you get a [new-deck surface](/services/composite-decks) for roughly half the cost of a full rebuild.
- **One or two isolated findings** — a soft board, a loose post, one flexy joist → **targeted repair** from the table above. Cheap, fast, done.
- **Anything touching the ledger, footings, or framing** → get a **professional inspection** before spending a dollar. These are the failures that don't announce themselves, and a $400 board swap on top of a rotting ledger is money burned.
- **Multiple structural findings on a 20-plus-year-old deck** → **replacement**. At that point each repair is a payment on a structure that's failing on schedule, and the math favors a rebuild — our [replacement cost guide](/blog/deck-replacement-cost-denver) has the full numbers, and [financing](/financing) can spread them out.

If you're weighing re-deck against rebuild, run both scenarios through our [deck cost calculator](/deck-cost-calculator) — the gap is usually smaller than people expect once tear-out is priced in.

## Why is fall the smart time to book repairs?

Two reasons, one about crews and one about physics.

**Crews:** deck season in Colorado runs hard from April through August. Come October, the same contractors who quoted you a six-week wait in June have open days on the calendar. Repairs are exactly the kind of work that fits into fall and winter — most fixes need only a dry stretch, not a warm one. (The same seasonal logic applies to full builds, as we cover in [the best time to build a deck in Colorado](/blog/best-time-to-build-deck-colorado).)

**Physics:** Denver's freeze-thaw cycles are a wedge. Water gets into a hairline check in a post, a gap behind loose flashing, or the soil under a marginal footing — then freezes, expands, and pries. A loose railing post in September is a dangerous one by April; a small ledger leak in October is a rot colony by spring. Every winter you wait, the repair bid grows.

Booking in the fall means the problem is fixed *before* winter works on it, usually at better availability and never at a seasonal premium.

## What should a repair quote include?

The same thing a build quote should: line items. A lump-sum "deck repair — $2,800" tells you nothing; an itemized quote tells you what's structural, what's cosmetic, and what can wait a year. It should also name the hardware — joist hangers, structural screws, post bases — because a fastener refresh done with the wrong screws is a repair you'll pay for twice.
