**A covered deck in Denver costs $6,000–$12,000 for an open pergola, $10,000–$22,000 for polycarbonate, and $15,000–$28,000 for a solid roof or pavilion added to a sound deck in 2026. Built together, a 16×20 composite deck under polycarbonate runs $23,000–$47,000 as one project.**

A cover is the upgrade that changes how much a Colorado deck gets used — shade in July, shelter in an afternoon storm, and a season that runs from March snow-showers into November. It's also where quotes diverge most wildly, because "covered deck" spans everything from an open pergola to a fully roofed outdoor room. Here are the real 2026 Denver-metro prices, and the engineering behind them.

## Covered deck prices by roof type

For a cover added to a new or structurally sound existing deck:

| Cover type | Typical installed cost | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| Open-rafter pergola (timber or aluminum) | $6,000–$12,000 | Sun |
| Polycarbonate roof cover | $10,000–$22,000 | Sun, hail, rain — keeps daylight |
| Adjustable louvered-roof pergola | $15,000–$30,000+ | Sun and rain, on demand |
| Solid roof / tongue-and-groove pavilion | $15,000–$28,000 | Everything; true outdoor room |
| Screened enclosure (added to a covered structure) | +$3,000–$8,000 | Bugs and wind |

Building deck and cover together? Add normal deck pricing — [$40–$70 per square foot installed](/blog/composite-deck-cost-denver) for capped composite — so a 16×20 composite deck ($13,000–$25,000) under a polycarbonate roof runs around **$23,000–$47,000 as one project**. Planning them together is cheaper and better-engineered than bolting a roof onto a deck that wasn't built for one: loads land on posts and footings sized for them from day one, instead of new caissons cut through a finished deck.

## What moves the number

**Span and posts.** A roof over a 12-foot deck on two posts is a different structure than one spanning 20 feet with an open corner. Longer spans mean bigger beams — aluminum spans farther than timber before needing a post — and every post means a frost-depth footing at **$150–$600**.

**The snow-load line.** This is the quiet difference between a Denver quote and an internet price. A flatland pergola kit is designed for sun; a Front Range cover is engineered for the standard **30 psf design snow load** — two feet of wet spring snow — and the wind that tries to lift it the rest of the year. Real engineering means 30–36-inch footings, heavier connections, and beams sized for loads a kit never contemplated. It's why our covers cost more than the box-store version, and why ours are [still standing after the storms](/services/pergolas-patio-covers) that flatten the kits.

**Roof material.** Open rafters are the least material and the least shelter. Multiwall polycarbonate — typically 8–16 mm twin- or triple-wall panels — lets daylight through while blocking essentially all UV and shrugging off the hail that is the number-one roof risk on the Front Range; our [polycarbonate covers guide](/blog/polycarbonate-roof-covers-guide) covers the panel grades. Tongue-and-groove wood ceilings under a solid roof give an indoor-quality finish overhead and the full outdoor-room effect.

**What's underneath.** A roof adds real load, so an existing deck gets a structural check first — sometimes reinforcement, occasionally the news that the deck itself is the project. Our [10-minute inspection guide](/deck-safety-inspection-guide) previews what we'll check, and the full retrofit math (new footings, reinforcement, permit) is in our guide to [adding a roof to an existing deck](/blog/add-roof-to-existing-deck).

**Permits.** A roofed structure is its own engineered, permitted build — figure **$150–$500 in fees** and a 1–3 week turnaround in most south-metro cities. A quote that doesn't mention the permit is telling you who they expect to deal with the county.

**Electrical.** Lights, ceiling fans, and infrared heaters are what turn a cover into a room. Wired during the build they're a modest line; retrofitted later they're an ordeal. Budget **$1,200–$3,000** for a lighting package, more with fans and heaters.

## When a cover pencils out

Here's how we'd think about the spend. An uncovered west-facing deck — under UV roughly 25% stronger than sea level — is genuinely comfortable maybe four hours a day in summer: mornings and after sunset. Shaded, it's usable all day; under a solid roof, it's usable in rain, hail season, and shoulder-month weather too. Homeowners tell us a cover doubled their real use of the space, which flips the per-square-foot math: a **$15,000** solid roof over a 320 sq ft deck adds all-day usability for about **$47 per square foot** — the same rate as the deck itself, for space that now works three seasons instead of a few hours a day.

The decision rules fall out of the table: if the only problem is sun, a **pergola** solves it for $6,000–$12,000 and anything more is over-buying. If hail and rain are in the picture but you want the light, **polycarbonate** is the middle path. If you want shoulder-season dinners, a ceiling fan, and a finished ceiling overhead, you're a **solid-roof** buyer.

If the budget says later, say so during design: we'll place footings and size framing so the roof can land in two years without rework. Phasing is a [financing strategy](/financing) in its own right, and it beats both waiting and over-borrowing.
