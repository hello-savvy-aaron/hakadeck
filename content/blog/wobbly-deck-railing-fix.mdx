**A wobbly railing is usually a loose post-to-rim connection, and re-anchoring it with through-bolts or tension hardware costs $150–$400 per post — often a single crew-day for the whole railing.** The exception worth knowing about: if the *framing* moves with the post, or the post base is rotting, the railing is telling you about a structural problem, and that needs an inspection before it needs hardware.

## Why a wobbly railing actually matters

The code numbers are blunt about what a guardrail is for. Any deck surface more than **30 inches above grade** requires one; it has to stand at least **36 inches tall**, keep openings under **4 inches**, and resist a **200-pound load in any direction** along the top rail. That last number is the one a wobbly railing fails. Two hundred pounds is an adult stumbling into it, a kid climbing it, a guest leaning back with a drink. A railing that rocks under your hand is nowhere near holding that — and on a second-story deck, the consequence of finding out is severe. Our [deck safety inspection guide](/deck-safety-inspection-guide) puts the railing shake-test first for a reason.

## What makes a railing come loose?

Three causes account for nearly all of it:

**The post-to-rim connection — the usual suspect.** Most wobbly railings trace back to where the post bolts to the deck frame. Wood shrinks and swells through Colorado's freeze-thaw cycles, lag screws work loose in their holes, and every lean on the rail levers the connection a little wider. Once there's play, every use makes it worse.

**A rotted post base.** Water sits where the post meets the decking, and after enough seasons the wood at the base goes soft. No amount of tightening fixes rot — the fastener has nothing solid to bite.

**Worked-loose balusters or rail sections.** Sometimes the posts are solid and it's the infill that rattles — balusters that have shrunk out of their fasteners or a rail section flexing at its brackets. Annoying, and a code problem if gaps have opened past 4 inches, but the cheapest of the three to fix.

## What does each fix cost?

Denver-metro ranges for [railing repair work](/services/deck-repair):

| Fix | Typical cost | When it's the answer |
|---|---|---|
| Re-anchor posts (through-bolts / tension hardware) | $150–$400 per post | Solid post, loose connection — the common case |
| Refit balusters or a rail section | $150–$500 | Posts firm, infill rattling |
| Full replacement — composite or basic metal | $30–$60 per linear foot | Rot or damage in multiple posts |
| Full replacement — aluminum | $60–$90 per linear foot | Same, with a slimmer profile |
| Full replacement — cable rail | $80–$130 per linear foot | Replacing anyway, want the view |
| Full replacement — glass | $100–$150+ per linear foot | Replacing anyway, want all of the view |

The right fix follows the diagnosis. Loose connections on sound wood get modern tension hardware and through-bolts — a far stronger connection than the original lag screws, and the whole job is usually done in a day. Rot in one post can mean a post swap; rot in several usually makes [full replacement](/services/railings) the better spend, since you're paying mobilization either way and a new railing resets the clock on every connection at once. ## When is a wobbly railing a structural problem?

Here's the honest caveat. Sometimes the railing is fine and the deck isn't. Grab the post and watch where the movement happens:

- **The rim joist flexes with the post.** The connection is tight — it's the framing behind it that's moving. That can mean a deteriorating rim joist or, worse, trouble at the ledger.
- **The post base is soft, dark, or crumbling.** Rot rarely confines itself politely to one post. Whatever wet the post base has likely been wetting the framing too.
- **Hardware won't stay tight.** If you (or a previous owner) have re-tightened the same post more than once, the wood has stopped holding thread. Bigger screws into compromised wood is not a fix.

Any of those means an inspection before anyone sells you railing hardware. On an older deck, the finding is sometimes that the frame is the real project — at which point the honest comparison is repair versus [replacement](/services/deck-replacement), and the [real numbers on that](/blog/deck-replacement-cost-denver) are worth seeing before you sink money into a railing bolted to a failing frame.

## Why fall is the time to do it

Railing-only jobs are quick — most are one crew-day, no excavation, no long permit cycle. That makes them ideal fall projects: crews have room in the schedule after the summer rush, and the railing gets solid *before* the icy season, when a grab at a loose rail on snowy stairs is exactly the scenario the 200-pound rule exists for.
