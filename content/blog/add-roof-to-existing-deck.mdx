**Yes, most existing decks can carry a roof — after a structural check, and often with new footings — and in the Denver metro the retrofit runs $6,000–$12,000 for an open pergola up to $15,000–$30,000+ for a louvered system or solid roof.** Here's what the job actually involves when the deck is already there.

## What does a roof over an existing deck cost?

| Cover type | Typical installed cost | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| Open-rafter pergola | $6,000–$12,000 | Sun |
| Polycarbonate roof | $10,000–$22,000 | Sun, hail, rain — keeps the daylight |
| Adjustable louvered pergola | $15,000–$30,000+ | Sun and rain, on demand |
| Solid roof / tongue-and-groove pavilion | $15,000–$28,000 | Everything; a true outdoor room |
| Screened enclosure (on a covered structure) | +$3,000–$8,000 | Bugs and wind |

Those are the same structure prices from our [full covered-deck cost guide](/blog/covered-deck-cost-denver) — the deeper dive on what moves each number. What's different about a retrofit is everything underneath, which is the rest of this post.

## Can my deck carry a roof?

This is the first question, and nobody can answer it from the driveway. A roof adds real load — dead load from the structure itself, and the standard **30 psf design snow load** a Front Range cover is engineered for, which is two feet of wet spring snow parked over your dinner table. Your deck was designed to hold people and furniture, not a building.

So the retrofit starts with a structural evaluation: are the posts sized and braced for the new load path, do the footings reach **30–36-inch frost depth** and have the bearing area for the added weight, and is the framing sound where the roof loads land? Sometimes the answer is yes as-is. Often it's yes with reinforcement — sistered beams, added posts, upgraded hardware. You can preview most of what we'll check with our [10-minute inspection guide](/deck-safety-inspection-guide).

**New footings are the retrofit's signature move.** Roof posts usually need their own caissons at **$150–$600 each**, and on an existing deck those get cut through the decking or set just outside the frame — done cleanly, the boards go back and you'd never know. For attached roofs, the house-side connection is its own engineered detail: a ledger or wall connection that transfers roof load into the house framing and stays flashed watertight. It's fussier than starting fresh, which is why building [deck and cover together](/blog/covered-deck-cost-denver) is cheaper when you have the choice — but a well-built existing deck is a fine foundation for a roof.

## What if the deck itself needs work first?

Sometimes the honest answer is that the deck is the project. If the evaluation turns up a failing ledger, rotted posts, or footings heaved out of position, bolting a roof on top makes a marginal structure worse — now with a snow load on it. In that case [repair](/services/deck-repair) comes first, or, if the framing is far enough gone, [replacement](/services/deck-replacement) — and the good news is a replacement deck gets designed for the roof from day one, which is the best version of this project anyway. We'd rather tell you that at the evaluation than discover it at the build.

## Which cover fixes your actual problem?

| The problem | The right cover | The budget |
|---|---|---|
| Sun only — July afternoons are brutal | Open-rafter pergola | $6,000–$12,000 |
| Hail and rain, but keep the light | Polycarbonate roof | $10,000–$22,000 |
| Want sun some days, shelter others | Louvered pergola | $15,000–$30,000+ |
| All-season outdoor room | Solid roof / pavilion | $15,000–$28,000 |

If the only problem is sun, a pergola solves it and anything more is over-buying. If hail — the number-one roof risk on the Front Range — and rain are in the picture but you don't want a dark deck, multiwall polycarbonate is the middle path; our [polycarbonate guide](/blog/polycarbonate-roof-covers-guide) breaks down the panel grades. And if the endgame is bug-free September dinners, a [screened enclosure on a solid roof](/blog/screened-porch-three-season-room-colorado) is the full outdoor-room play.

## Do I need a permit for a deck roof?

Yes — a roofed structure is its own engineered, permitted build in every jurisdiction we work in. Figure **$150–$500 in fees** and a **1–3 week plan review**, with the engineering showing the 30 psf snow load and wind uplift resistance an inspector expects to see. A cover quote that doesn't mention the permit is telling you who they expect to handle the county. Our [permits guide](/deck-permits-south-denver-metro) covers what each south-metro city wants; [we handle the whole process](/services/pergolas-patio-covers) on every cover we build.

## What should I add while the crew is there?

Electrical. Lights, a ceiling fan, an infrared heater — these are what turn a cover into a room, and they're a modest line item while the structure is open versus an ordeal retrofitted later. Budget **$1,200–$3,000** for a lighting package, more with fans and heat. If the budget is tight this year, wire the boxes now and add fixtures later — phasing like that is a [financing strategy](/financing) in its own right.

## Why build the cover in fall?

Two reasons. First, the calendar: cover crews have room in the fall between the summer build season and the spring rush, so the permit review and the build both move faster. Second, the payoff: a cover finished in October is standing over your deck when spring hail season opens — which is precisely the storm season the polycarbonate and solid-roof options exist for. Build it now, and next May's hail bounces off the roof instead of the patio furniture.
