Here's the number that should start every hot-tub-on-a-deck conversation: a typical 6-person spa weighs about 800 pounds empty — and **5,500 to 6,500 pounds filled and occupied**. That's two SUVs, parked on a 7-by-7 footprint, permanently. A standard residential deck is designed for a **40-pounds-per-square-foot live load**; a filled tub on 49 square feet imposes **110–130 psf** — roughly three times the design load. Put one on a deck that wasn't engineered for it and the deck will tell you, slowly and then suddenly.

The math by spa size:

| Spa | Footprint | Filled + occupied | Load on deck |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2–3 person (~5.5 ft round) | ~24 sq ft | ~3,000 lbs | **~125 psf** |
| 6-person (7×7 ft) | 49 sq ft | 5,500–6,500 lbs | **110–130 psf** |
| Swim spa (8×16 ft) | 128 sq ft | 20,000–25,000 lbs | **160–200 psf** |

Note that smaller doesn't mean lighter per square foot — the psf barely moves. And a swim spa on a deck is a bridge-engineering problem; those almost always belong on grade. We build hot tub decks regularly across the metro, and the engineering is completely solvable. It just has to be *done*, not assumed.

## The three ways to support a tub

**On grade, deck built around it.** The tub sits on a reinforced concrete pad at ground level — most spa manufacturers spec a **4-inch reinforced slab minimum**, 6 inches for swim spas — and the deck wraps it, flush with the tub rim or stepping around it. This is the structural freebie: the pad carries the weight, the deck carries people. It's our default recommendation when the yard allows it, and it usually produces the best design too, since a rim-flush tub is far easier to get into than one you climb up to.

**On the deck, engineered for it.** When the tub genuinely needs to be on the deck — walk-outs where grade is a story down, or a layout that demands it — the framing under the tub footprint gets designed for the real load: joists at **12 inches on center or tighter** with spans roughly half of what standard tables allow, doubled or tripled beams, and additional frost-depth footings placed directly under the tub zone (each footing **$150–600**, same as any deck footing — there are just more of them, closer together). This isn't a beefed-up version of normal framing; it's a different structure for that bay, calculated, and it's exactly the kind of thing that goes on the [permit drawings](/deck-permits-south-denver-metro).

**On an existing deck — after an honest inspection.** The most dangerous version is the impulse buy: tub delivered Saturday, placed on a 15-year-old deck that was never designed for a fraction of the load. Before a tub goes on any existing deck we inspect the structure the way we would for a [repair assessment](/services/deck-repair) — framing, footings, and ledger — and give you a written answer: fine as-is, fine after reinforcement, or ground-pad time. Reinforcement is often very doable; guessing is not.

## Design details that make or break it

**Access and clearance.** Spa service techs need panel access, usually on a specific side — typically **18–24 inches of clearance** at the equipment bay. Plan it during design; a tub serviced by tearing out decking is a tub that never gets serviced.

**Rim height.** Sunken or semi-sunken installs (deck at rim height) look best and enter easiest, but need drainage and airflow in the vault so the tub isn't sitting in a humid pit. We frame vault walls with ventilation and a drainage path, always — and that drainage path matters twice a year, because a 400-gallon tub gets drained for water changes and all of it has to go somewhere that isn't your framing.

**Privacy and wind.** Colorado hot-tubbing is a winter sport, and wind is the enemy of a 104-degree soak. Slat [privacy screens](/services/pergolas-patio-covers) and partial roofs turn an exposed tub into a year-round one — some of our favorite builds pair a tub vault with a louvered pergola overhead.

**Power and code.** A spa circuit is a **240V, 50–60 amp GFCI-protected run** with its own disconnect — code requires it within sight of the tub and at least 5 feet from the water. Budget **$1,000–$2,500** for a typical run from the panel, more if the panel needs upgrading. We coordinate the electrical rough-in during framing so nothing gets trenched through a finished yard later.

**Surface choice.** Wet feet, dripped chlorine, and constant splash-zone moisture argue hard for [capped composite or PVC](/services/composite-decks) over wood around a tub — no refinishing a splash zone every summer, no splinters on bare feet.

## What about pool decks?

Same physics, different scale. We build composite decks and surrounds for above-ground pools and spas — the wrap-around platform that turns an above-ground pool from an appliance into a place. In-ground pool surrounds in concrete or pavers are a different trade (our [concrete vs. pavers guide](/blog/concrete-vs-pavers-guide) covers that decision), but where the answer is a raised wood or composite structure, that's deck work, and it gets the same engineering attention as a tub vault: real footings, moisture-immune surfaces, and framing sized for wet loads.

## What it costs

A hot-tub-ready deck carries a premium only in the reinforced zone — figure **$1,500–$4,000 above standard framing** for the engineered bay, footings, and vault work, on top of normal deck pricing of **$40–$70 per square foot** installed. A ground-pad install is cheaper still: the pad typically runs **$1,000–$2,500** and the deck around it prices as a normal deck. Electrical is its own **$1,000–$2,500** line either way.
