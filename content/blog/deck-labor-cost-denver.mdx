Homeowners comparing deck bids eventually ask the blunt question: *how much of this is labor?* Fair question. Here's the honest anatomy of a Denver-metro deck quote in 2026 — and why the labor line is the worst place to bargain-hunt.

## The short answer

On a professionally built composite deck in the Denver metro, **labor typically runs 40–60% of the total project cost** — professional installation adds roughly **$15–$35 per square foot** on top of materials, within the $40–$70/sq ft installed price. By deck size, the labor share alone works out to:

| Size | Labor share (at $15–$35/sq ft) |
|---|---|
| 12×16 (192 sq ft) | $2,900–$6,700 |
| 16×20 (320 sq ft) | $4,800–$11,200 |
| 20×24 (480 sq ft) | $7,200–$16,800 |

A useful cross-check: composite boards run **$8–$14 per sq ft**, all-in materials (framing, footings, hardware, railing) land around **$20–$30 per sq ft** — then installation adds 50–100% on top. A $12,000 materials list is an $18,000–$24,000 finished deck.

If a bid's labor number is dramatically below that, it isn't a bargain — it's a preview. Something in the schedule, the crew, or the parts of the build you can't see is making up the difference.

## What that labor actually buys

A deck is a small engineered structure attached to your house, and most of the skilled hours go into the parts that disappear from view:

- **Layout and footings.** Digging 4–8 holes to Colorado frost depth (30–36 inches), setting forms, pouring and anchoring — at **$150–$600 per footing installed**, the foundation phase is slow, heavy, and completely invisible in the finished photos.
- **The ledger.** Attaching and flashing the deck to the house correctly is the single most safety-critical connection on the build. Botched ledgers are the classic cause of deck collapse — and the classic shortcut of cheap labor.
- **Framing.** Beams, joists, blocking, hardware. Board-straightness and hidden-fastener quality on the surface are decided here, one level down.
- **Surface and railings.** The part you see — and at 36-inch code height with sub-4-inch baluster spacing, railing work is fussier than the field boards. Installed railing runs **$30–$90 per linear foot**, and most of that spread is hours, not aluminum. Stairs price at **$150–$400 per step** for the same reason.
- **Permits and inspections.** Drawings, submission ($150–$500 in fees, south metro), scheduling, and standing on site for all three inspections — footing holes, rough framing, final. [We handle this end to end](/blog/deck-permits-centennial-arapahoe-douglas); crews that skip it aren't cheaper, they're just transferring risk to you.

Put percentages on it and the picture flips from what most people assume: on a finished composite deck, the substructure and its labor run about **38%** of the bill, decking-and-railing labor about **30%**, railing material about **12%** — and the boards themselves only about **20%**. The material you agonize over is a fifth of the check; the hours are half of it.

## The multipliers are mostly labor

The three factors that legitimately move a bid are labor factors:

- **Height** (walk-out / second story): **+20–40%** — taller posts, staging, railing work at height.
- **Access** (tight side yards, no machine access): **+10–15%** — everything moves by wheelbarrow.
- **Complexity** (multi-level, angles, curves): **+15–25%** — corners cost framing hours, not lumber.

These stack. It's how two honest bids on 320 sq ft decks can land at $14,000 and $27,000 without either builder being wrong — the jobs aren't the same job.

## Can you save the labor by doing it yourself?

Arithmetically, yes — DIY recaptures the $15–$35/sq ft, which on a 16×20 deck is **$4,800–$11,200**. The honest accounting is less generous: you'll buy or rent an auger, saws, and hardware a crew already owns; materials come at retail instead of contractor pricing; and a first deck takes most people 6–10 full weekends against a crew's one to two weeks. The decision rule we'd give a friend: DIY a ground-level, rectangular, detached deck if you enjoy the work — our [DIY deck building checklist](/diy-deck-building-checklist) is the punch list — and hire out anything elevated, attached to the house, or carrying a roof. The ledger and the footings are exactly where the labor money goes, and exactly where amateur work fails.

## Why Denver labor prices where it does

Colorado's structural requirements are heavier than the national average — frost-depth footings, framing for a 30 psf design snow load, wind exposure in the southern metro, and 35–90 freeze-thaw cycles a year working on every fastener — and experienced deck carpenters are in demand across a metro that builds outdoor living ten months a year. Skilled crews price accordingly, and the difference shows up twenty years later in a deck that's still square, still tight, and still standing on its original footings.

## How to compare bids on labor

Don't compare the number; compare what it covers. Ask each builder: Who actually swings the hammers — employees or the cheapest available sub? Is the ledger flashed and bolted, or lagged and caulked? Are footings dug to frost depth or set on pads? Is the permit pulled by them or left to you? Our [questions-to-ask checklist](/questions-to-ask-your-deck-builder) runs the full list.
