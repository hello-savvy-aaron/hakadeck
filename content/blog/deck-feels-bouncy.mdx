**A bouncy deck usually isn't about to fall down — it's telling you the framing is flexing more than it should. The common causes are joists spanned near their limit, missing blocking between joists, hardware working loose, or a post going soft at grade. Most fixes run $300–$1,200.**

Bounce is a stiffness problem. Strength is whether the deck can carry the load; stiffness is whether it moves while doing it. A deck can be safely strong and still trampoline under a full patio table — but the same symptom can also be an early warning on a connection that matters, so it's worth diagnosing rather than shrugging off.

## What makes a deck bounce?

Four causes account for nearly all of it:

- **Undersized or overspanned joists.** A 2x8 asked to span 14 feet will hold you — and flex like a diving board doing it. Common on older decks and on builds that skipped the span tables.
- **Missing blocking.** Blocking is the short lumber between joists that makes them act as one system instead of individual boards. Decks built without it feel springy and often wobble side-to-side too.
- **Failing hardware.** Denver's freeze-thaw cycles back fasteners out a little every winter. Loose joist hangers and post connections turn a tight frame into a loose one — movement you feel as bounce and hear as creaks.
- **Post rot at grade.** A wood post that meets soil or sits on a shallow footing softens from the bottom up. The deck settles onto a slowly failing support, and the whole structure gains play.

## What does each fix cost?

| Fix | Typical cost (Denver metro, 2026) |
|---|---|
| Blocking rows added between joists | $300–$800 |
| Joist sistering (new joist fastened alongside) | $150–$400 per joist |
| Post replacement with frost-depth footing | $500–$1,200 each |

Blocking is the best value in deck repair — a day's work that transforms how a springy deck feels. Sistering fixes the overspanned-joist problem without reframing, and a proper post replacement includes a new footing to Colorado's **30–36-inch frost depth**, which is why it costs what it does. For context on the full repair menu, see our [deck repair service page](/services/deck-repair).

While a crew is under there, a hardware refresh — re-driving hangers, replacing corroded fasteners with structural screws — is cheap insurance, and it's part of what a good [inspection checklist](/deck-safety-inspection-guide) covers.

## When is bounce dangerous?

**Location is the tell.** Bounce in the middle of the deck is a stiffness problem. Bounce, sag, or any sense of movement **near the house is a different animal** — that's the ledger zone, the bolted connection that carries about half the deck's weight, and ledger failure is the leading cause of catastrophic deck collapse. If the deck moves at the house side, or you can see a gap opening between deck and wall, stop diagnosing and [get it inspected now](/contact).

Age stacks the odds too. A springy five-year-old deck probably needs blocking. A springy twenty-year-old deck may be flexing because several things are wearing out at once — and at that point it's worth an honest conversation about [repair versus replacement](/services/deck-replacement) rather than fixing symptoms one at a time. A new composite frame, for what it's worth, is engineered stiff from day one — bounce is one of the complaints a [modern composite build](/services/composite-decks) simply doesn't have.
