**A deck post that's soft, crumbly, or losing its shape at the base is rotting because water is sitting against the wood there and never drying out — almost always from soil contact, a concrete collar that traps rainwater, or a missing standoff hardware.** Replacing a post and footing together runs roughly $500–$1,200 in the Denver metro. The fix is often smaller than people fear — but a post that's lost real wood needs shoring before anyone touches it.

## Why do deck posts rot at the base?

Four causes, usually at least two working together:

- **Posts buried directly in soil.** Wood in constant contact with soil wicks moisture nonstop. Even pressure-treated, ground-contact-rated lumber is only protected to a certain chemical depth — the protection wears thin at the surface, and decay starts from the outside in. The IRC requires this kind of ground-contact treatment for any wood embedded in soil or concrete that supports a structure.
- **Posts set in a concrete collar.** Pouring concrete around a post is often worse than soil. Concrete is porous and shaped like a cup — it catches rainwater and holds it against the grain longer than open ground, which is why "set in concrete" posts often rot exactly at the concrete line.
- **A missing or failed post base.** Code is specific: it calls for roughly a 1-inch standoff between the end grain of the post and concrete (or about 6 inches above exposed soil with a moisture barrier), so water physically can't bridge to the end grain. Hardware makers like Simpson Strong-Tie build standoff post bases around exactly that gap. A post set flush on a footing or toe-nailed to a buried pier with no standoff skips that protection.
- **Frost heave.** Colorado's freeze-thaw cycle moves the ground every winter. A footing poured too shallow lifts and settles unevenly, which can crack the footing, tip a post out of plumb, or open a gap at the base that collects water.

## Surface rot or structural loss — how do you tell?

Use a screwdriver on all four faces and the end grain at the base, the same probe test used on deck boards and joists. **Surface rot** is confined to a thin outer shell — scrape away the soft layer and there's firm wood underneath. It's worth treating, not an emergency. **Structural loss** is when the blade sinks an inch or more with no resistance, the post visibly narrows compared to its neighbors, or you can wiggle it at the base — load-bearing wood is gone.

One rule either way: don't rock, bounce, or load-test a post you already suspect is bad. If it's the only thing holding that corner up, testing it is how it fails.

## What should you check today?

| Check | What you're looking for |
|---|---|
| Base of every post | Soft, dark, or crumbly wood at or just below grade |
| Screwdriver probe, all sides + end grain | Blade sinks in easily, especially near the ground or concrete line |
| Plumb | A post leaning or out of square with its footing — possible heave or lost bearing |
| Standoff hardware | A visible gap between wood and concrete, versus a post sitting flush or buried |
| Grading around the post | Soil, mulch, or debris piled against the base, holding moisture there |

## How is it fixed, and what does it cost?

The fix depends on how much of the post is actually gone:

- **Surface rot, sound core** — clean out the soft wood, and if there's no standoff hardware, add one. This is the cheapest outcome and often rides along with a general [hardware refresh](/blog/deck-repair-cost-denver).
- **Structural loss at the base** — the deck gets temporarily shored first, with jacks or temporary posts carrying the load off the damaged one, before any cutting starts. This isn't a step to improvise — getting the sequence wrong drops a corner of the deck.
- **Post replacement on a sound footing** — if the existing footing is intact and at depth, the rotted post is cut out and a new one set on an elevated standoff base, keeping it off the concrete.
- **Full post-and-footing replacement** — when the footing itself is undersized, cracked, or shallower than the local frost line, the old footing comes out and a new one is poured to depth, with a standoff base and new post set on top. The AWC's prescriptive deck guide requires footings to reach at least 12 inches below grade or below frost line, whichever is deeper — commonly 30–36 inches across the Denver metro, but confirmed with your building department.

Either version of post-and-footing work runs **$500–$1,200 per post** in the Denver metro in 2026.
