**Cupped or splitting deck boards mean moisture is moving through the wood unevenly — and in Colorado's sun and freeze-thaw, that process only runs one direction.** A few bad boards is a cheap swap. Widespread cupping on an aging deck is the surface telling you it's done, and the smart response is usually a re-deck, not a board-by-board fight you can't win.

## Why does Colorado do this to wood decks?

A deck board is wood cut flat and asked to stay that way outdoors, and the Front Range is close to the hardest place in the country to ask it. The sun here carries roughly **25% more UV than sea level**, and it shows up **300+ days a year**. UV breaks down lignin — the glue holding wood fibers together — at the surface, while the sun bakes the top face dry and the shaded underside stays damp. Different moisture on the two faces makes the board curl, edges up: that's cupping.

Then winter takes its turn. Water sits in the wood, freezes, expands, thaws, and repeats — dozens of cycles a season along the Front Range. Boards swell wet and shrink dry, over and over, until the wood fibers give up along the grain: that's splitting. A stain or sealer slows all of this; nothing stops it.

## Is cupping actually a problem, or just ugly?

It's a problem, and a compounding one. A cupped board is a shallow trough, and a trough **holds water** — so the board that cupped because of moisture now stores moisture against itself and its fasteners after every rain and snowmelt. That's the on-ramp to rot. Splits are worse for the hardware: a crack that reaches a screw gives water a channel straight to the fastener and the joist below it, and a split board **swallows fasteners** — heads sink, shanks lose their bite, and the board starts working loose underfoot. If boards are moving when you walk the deck, it's worth running the checks in our [deck safety inspection guide](/deck-safety-inspection-guide), because loose decking and tired framing often travel together.

## Replace boards, re-deck, or replace the deck?

The triage comes down to two questions: how widespread is the surface damage, and what shape is the framing in?

| Situation | The right fix | Denver-metro cost |
|---|---|---|
| A few cupped or split boards, deck otherwise sound | Board swap | $150–$500 wood; $200–$600 composite-matched |
| Widespread cupping, wood deck 15–20+ years old, framing sound | Re-deck in capped composite | $20–$35 per sq ft |
| Surface *and* framing failing | Full replacement | $40–$70 per sq ft |

**A few bad boards** is a straightforward [repair](/services/deck-repair) — pull them, check the joists underneath while they're open, screw down new stock. Cheap, quick, done.

**Widespread cupping** is where homeowners burn money slowly. Replacing boards one at a time on a 15–20-year-old wood surface is paying repair prices, repeatedly, for a replacement outcome — every board you didn't swap this year is on the same clock. If the framing underneath is sound (and it often is; joists live in the shade and dry out between storms), the sweet-spot answer is a **re-deck**: strip the surface, keep the frame, and lay [capped composite](/services/composite-decks) at **$20–$35 per square foot** — a brand-new deck surface for roughly **half the cost of a full rebuild**.

**Framing gone too** — rot at the ledger, soft joists, posts past saving — and a re-deck would be new boards on a dying frame. That's [full replacement](/services/deck-replacement) territory at **$40–$70 per square foot**, and the honest math on when that line gets crossed is laid out in our [deck replacement cost guide](/blog/deck-replacement-cost-denver).

## Why composite is the honest answer to cupping

Cupping and splitting are *wood* behaviors — they exist because wood absorbs and releases water. Capped composite doesn't, so there's nothing to curl and nothing to crack. The board you install is the shape it stays, through every freeze-thaw winter and every 95-degree July.

The honest caveat, because there is one: composite isn't magic in high-altitude sun. Early-generation boards could chalk or fade noticeably; modern capped boards can still lighten slightly over many years. The difference is that today's caps are engineered for exactly this exposure, the change is cosmetic rather than structural, and the fade is [covered in writing by manufacturer warranties](/warranty) — decades long from the brands we install. A cupped cedar board holds water against your framing; a composite board that's a half-shade lighter in year fifteen holds a dinner party.
