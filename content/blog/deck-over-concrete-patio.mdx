**Yes — you can usually build a deck over a concrete patio without removing the slab. A sleeper system over sound concrete costs $25–$40 per square foot installed in the 2026 Denver metro, versus $40–$70 ground-up, because there are no footings to dig. The slab just has to be stable and drain away from the house.**

It's one of the most common backyard questions in the metro's older neighborhoods: there's a concrete patio off the back door — cracked, sunken at one corner, hot as a skillet in July — and you'd rather have a deck. In most cases the slab you're tired of becomes the foundation for the deck you want.

## How do deck sleepers over concrete work?

The standard approach is a **sleeper system**: pressure-treated framing laid over the concrete on spacers, leveled to correct slope and dips, with composite decking fastened on top. The slab keeps doing what concrete does best — bearing load — while the sleepers create a drainage gap and a flat, consistent surface.

What makes it last:

- **Sleeper height.** Most composite manufacturers require at least **1.5 inches of free air space** under the boards for ventilation — a 2×4 laid flat hits that number exactly. Where the slab dips, sleepers get shimmed or tapered to bring everything to one plane.
- **Sleeper spacing.** Sleepers run **16 inches on center** for perpendicular decking, tightening to **12 inches** for diagonal layouts or boards whose manufacturer requires it.
- **Drainage.** The sleepers run parallel to the slab's slope so water sheds underneath. A patio poured to code already slopes **1/8 to 1/4 inch per foot** away from the house; the sleeper system preserves that path instead of damming it.
- **Total buildup.** Sleeper plus a composite board (about **0.94 inch** thick) adds roughly **2.5 inches** to the slab height — the number that decides whether the finished surface clears your door threshold.

The whole sleeper spec on one screen:

| Deck sleepers over concrete | The standard |
|---|---|
| Sleeper size | 2×4 laid flat, for the 1.5 inches of air space composite needs |
| Spacing | 16 inches on center; 12 inches for diagonal boards |
| Lumber | Ground-contact-rated pressure-treated |
| Isolation | Joist tape or a composite shim between wood and concrete |
| Fastening | Concrete screws or anchors about every 24 inches |
| Direction | Parallel to the slab's slope, so water runs out underneath |
| Added height | About 2.5 inches with a standard composite board |

## The step-down alternative: low-profile framing

Where the patio sits well below the door — a **4-to-8-inch step-down** is common on older homes — there's a second option: a **low-profile floating deck** framed over and around the slab on 2×6 joists, bringing the finished surface flush with the interior floor. Framing plus decking adds about **6 to 8 inches**, erasing the step-down and turning the patio from a landing into a room. ## What the slab has to pass

Concrete with surface cracks, stains, or spalling is fine — cosmetics don't matter once it's covered. What we check is movement and drainage:

- **Heaving or settling.** Colorado's clay soils and **35–90 freeze-thaw cycles a year** move slabs. Modest, stable settling gets corrected in the sleeper heights; a slab actively heaving each season will telegraph the movement upward.
- **Drainage.** The slab needs to shed water away from the house — if it has settled into a back-slope toward the foundation, that gets fixed first, not covered up.
- **Height math.** Slab plus sleepers plus decking has to land somewhere sensible: below the door threshold, below any siding or weep line, and in step with existing stairs. Usually it does; occasionally it dictates the low-profile framing route instead.

## What makes it last

Three specs separate a twenty-year over-slab deck from a five-year one:

- **Ground-contact lumber.** Sleepers live in the splash zone between decking and concrete, so they get **ground-contact-rated pressure-treated stock**, plus a strip of joist tape or a composite shim isolating wood from wet concrete.
- **Anchoring.** Sleepers get fastened to the slab with concrete screws or anchors roughly **every 24 inches**, so the assembly moves as one plane, not board by board.
- **Open edges.** The perimeter stays vented — solid skirting that seals the cavity turns a drainage gap into a terrarium. Vented skirting or an open picture-frame edge keeps air moving under the boards.

One limit: a sleeper deck carries people, not roofs. If a pergola or cover is in the plan, its posts need their own frost-depth footings drilled through or placed beside the slab — the slab's 4 inches of unreinforced concrete isn't a foundation for point loads. Plan the footings now even if the roof comes later.

## What it costs

Building over a sound slab is one of the rare deck scenarios that's *cheaper* than the standard build: the foundation already exists, so there are no footings to dig and pour — and footings run **$150–600 each**, four to eight per conventional deck. The three routes, 2026 Denver metro, installed composite:

| Route | Installed cost | When it applies |
|---|---|---|
| Sleeper system over sound slab | **$25–$40 / sq ft** | Slab stable, height math works |
| Low-profile deck framed over/around slab | **$35–$55 / sq ft** | Big step-down, or slab partly unstable |
| Demo slab + ground-up deck | **$40–$70 / sq ft** + **$2–$6 / sq ft** removal | Slab actively heaving, or grade change needed |

Compare that to [$40–$70 for a full ground-up composite deck](/blog/composite-deck-cost-denver).
