**Composite costs more up front — $40–$70 per square foot installed versus $20–$35 for pressure-treated pine in Denver — but wins over ten years. A 320 sq ft wood deck at about $9,000 needs $3,000–5,000 in restaining over a decade; the same deck in capped composite at roughly $16,000 needs soap and water, and past year ten composite is ahead.**

Picking a decking material is one of the biggest decisions in a deck project. The surface you choose sets the installed rate — from **$20–35 per square foot** for pressure-treated pine to **$50–85** for PVC — and dictates how many weekends you lose to maintenance. Here are the numbers, the trade-offs, and a decision tree to help you pick.

## The Two Camps

**Natural wood** means pressure-treated pine, cedar, redwood, and tropical hardwoods like Ipe and Cumaru — real grain, lower entry cost.

**Composite** is a manufactured board of wood fibers and recycled plastic wrapped in a protective polymer "cap"; modern PVC and mineral-based boards drop the wood content entirely. The pitch: wood's look, plastic's lifespan.

## What It Costs

Denver-metro 2026 ranges, material plus installation. Composite boards alone run **$8–14 per square foot**; labor adds **$15–35 per square foot** — roughly 40–60% of the total.

| Material | Installed / sq ft | Lifespan | Maintenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure-treated pine | $20–$35 | 10–15 yrs | Stain/seal every 2–3 yrs |
| Cedar | $25–$40 | 15–20 yrs | Stain/seal every 2–3 yrs |
| Redwood | $30–$45 | 20–25 yrs | Stain/seal every 2–3 yrs |
| Tropical hardwood (Ipe, Cumaru) | $45–$75 | 25–50 yrs | Oil annually (or let it gray) |
| Capped composite (Trex, TimberTech, Fiberon) | $40–$70 | 25–30 yrs | Wash 1–2x per year |
| Mineral-based composite (Deckorators) | $40–$70 | 30+ yrs | Wash 1–2x per year |
| PVC / cellular vinyl (AZEK, Wolf) | $50–$85 | 30–50 yrs | Wash 1–2x per year |
| Aluminum (Wahoo, AridDek) | $50–$90 | 50+ yrs | Rinse only |

For a typical 16×20 (320 sq ft) deck with railing, that's roughly **$6,500–11,000** in pressure-treated, **$13,000–25,000** in capped composite, and **$16,000–27,000** in PVC. For calibration: the 2025 Zonda Cost vs Value report puts the national-average 16×20 composite deck addition at about **$25,000** (wood about **$18,000**) — with wood recouping ~82% at resale and composite ~68%. ## The 10-Year Math

Sticker price isn't the whole story. That pressure-treated deck wants a clean-and-restain every 2–3 years — **$400–900 per round** hired out — plus board and fastener repairs as UV and freeze-thaw do their work, on a surface that's aging anyway. The composite version just needs soap and water, and the day-one price gap narrows year by year.

## The Colorado Factor

The comparison tilts harder toward low-maintenance materials here than in most markets: at the south metro's **5,800–6,200 feet**, UV runs about **25% stronger** than sea level, so a wood deck rated "re-stain every 3 years" realistically needs it every **18–24 months**. Add **35–90 freeze-thaw cycles a year** working water into every screw hole, and wood ages faster here than the national averages suggest. The [full Colorado materials guide](/composite-vs-hardwood-decking-colorado) goes deeper.

## What's New in 2026

**Cooler-surface technology.** A standard dark composite board in direct Colorado sun can hit **~160°F on a 90°F day**. TimberTech's CoolDeck and Trex's Transcend Lineage now run **30–35°F cooler** — worth it for dark tones facing south or west.

**Mineral-based composites.** Deckorators Voyage and Vault replace wood flour with a mineral core — lighter, stronger, no moisture uptake, rated for ground-contact installs most composites can't.

**Higher recycled content.** Trex boards are roughly **95% recycled material** — often outranking tropical hardwoods on sustainability, which carry deforestation concerns.

**Realistic grain.** Four-sided embossing and multi-tonal streaking have closed most of the visual gap — premium capped composite is hard to distinguish from stained hardwood a few feet back.

**Hidden fasteners as default.** Almost every composite line now ships with a grooved-edge clip system — no screw heads, no exposed holes for freeze-thaw to exploit.

## Decision Tree

Run through these in order. Stop at the first one that fits.

**1. Is your total budget under $12,000 for a standard 300–400 sq ft deck?**
Pressure-treated pine at **$20–35 per square foot**. Plan to re-stain it every other year.

**2. Do you want real wood grain and you're willing to maintain it?**
Cedar or redwood balances looks and cost. Ipe or Cumaru for a 30-year deck if you don't mind the price or weight.

**3. Will you sell the house in the next 5–7 years?**
Entry capped composite (Trex Enhance, TimberTech EDGE, Fiberon Good Life). Strong resale appeal, zero maintenance during your ownership, bottom of the $40–70 range.

**4. Forever home, or you hate yard work?**
Premium PVC (TimberTech AZEK, Wolf) or mineral-based composite. Highest upfront cost, lowest lifetime cost, warranties from 50 years to lifetime.

**5. Full sun and you want dark colors?**
Request a cool-surface capstock line. Don't let anyone sell you a standard dark composite for a south-facing deck — that's the 160°F board.

**6. Coastal, fire-zone, or rooftop project?**
Aluminum. It costs more and it's still the right tool.

## A Few Trade-offs

Composite isn't perfect. It scratches (especially older uncapped lines), dark colors get hot without cool-surface tech, and you can't sand and refinish it like wood. If a board fails in year 18, you're matching a discontinued color.

Wood isn't doomed either — a well-maintained Ipe deck outlasts most composites, and no manufactured board matches weathered cedar or oiled hardwood. If you enjoy spring deck-prep, wood rewards the effort.
