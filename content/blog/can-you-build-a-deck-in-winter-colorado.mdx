**Yes — decks get built straight through Colorado winters, and the physics is more cooperative than most homeowners assume.** Footings, concrete, and framing all work in the cold with standard practices; the trade-off is a modest weather buffer in exchange for quiet permit counters, open crew calendars, and a deck that's finished before the first warm weekend.

This is the operational companion to our post on [the best time to build a deck in Colorado](/blog/best-time-to-build-deck-colorado) — that one is about *when to sign*; this one is about how the build itself actually works once the temperature drops.

## Don't the footings freeze?

No — and this is the part that surprises people. Deck footings in Colorado go below the **30–36-inch frost line** in every season, because that's the whole point of frost depth: soil below that line doesn't freeze. Winter digging through the frozen top layer is slower than October digging, but slower is not impossible — it's a few extra hours of excavation, not a canceled project.

Concrete cures fine in cold weather with standard practices the industry has used for decades: curing blankets over the fresh pour, mix adjustments for temperature, and timing pours for the right window. This isn't exotic — it's how commercial construction runs all winter in Denver.

## What does winter actually change on a build?

Less than you'd think, and mostly at the margins:

| Task | Summer | Winter reality |
|---|---|---|
| Permit review | 4–8 weeks in the spring pile | 1–3 weeks, no backlog |
| Footing excavation | Straightforward digging | Slower through frozen top layer |
| Concrete pour | Standard cure | Blankets and mix adjustments — routine |
| Framing | No temperature sensitivity | No temperature sensitivity |
| Composite install | Boards near full expansion | Boards contracted — gapping set for install-day temp; done on dry days |
| Crew availability | Booked weeks out | Calendar room |

Two rows deserve a closer look.

**Framing doesn't care about cold.** Lumber, structural screws, and joist hangers go together the same at 20°F as at 80°F. There is no framing-quality penalty for a January build.

**Composite is where the pro detail lives.** [Composite boards](/services/composite-decks) contract in cold and expand in heat, so the gap between boards has to be set for the temperature *on install day* — a board gapped for January will be tight by July if you space it like it's summer. Manufacturers publish the numbers; pros use them; DIYers routinely don't, and the result shows up six months later. In single-digit temps, handling also gets more careful — boards are more brittle cold — and installs wait for dry days.

## What actually pauses a winter build?

Two things, honestly: **active snow on build days** and **hard-frozen ground slowing excavation**. Neither cancels a project — they stretch it. The right way to plan a winter build is a weather buffer, not a weather cancellation: figure some built-in slack for storm days, the same way you'd figure afternoon-thunderstorm days into a July build. Our [process](/process) builds that buffer into the schedule up front, so a snowy week is an expected event, not a surprise.

## What do you get in exchange?

Three things that spring builders don't:

1. **Quiet permit counters.** Winter reviews run 1–3 weeks with no spring backlog. The same submittal in April joins everyone else's April idea.
2. **Crew calendar room.** Winter is when the schedule has air in it, which means your project starts when it's supposed to instead of six weeks after.
3. **Sequencing.** A deck signed in December is done before the first warm weekend — instead of being a napkin sketch during one. If you're still working out budget, the [Denver deck cost guide](/deck-cost-guide-denver) has current numbers to plan against.

## What about the small stuff?

Winter is prime season for it. [Repairs](/services/deck-repair), [railing swaps](/services/railings), and [patio covers](/services/pergolas-patio-covers) are one-to-two-week projects that fit neatly between storms — most need only a dry stretch, not a warm one. If your fall walk-through turned up a wobbly post or a soft board, winter is when that work gets scheduled fast instead of joining a spring waiting list.
